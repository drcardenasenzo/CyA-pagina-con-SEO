Inheritance in Argentina for Foreign Heirs

Living abroad does not by itself prevent an heir from asserting rights in Argentina, but jurisdiction, applicable law and the type of asset must be analysed first.

Dr. Cárdenas Enzo Javier
T° 153 F° 712 CPACF

Short answer

A foreign or non-resident heir may assert inheritance rights in Argentina, but the required route depends principally on the deceased’s last domicile, the location and type of each asset, any will or foreign probate proceeding, and the result sought. Argentine courts have jurisdiction over Argentine real estate for that property, and Argentine law applies to it. Other assets require a separate jurisdiction and applicable-law analysis.

Jurisdiction and applicable law are different questions

Jurisdiction identifies which court may act; applicable law identifies which legal rules govern the succession. Under Civil and Commercial Code Article 2643, courts at the deceased’s last domicile have succession jurisdiction, while Argentine courts may act regarding real estate situated in Argentina. Under Article 2644, the law of the deceased’s domicile at death generally governs, but Argentine law governs real estate situated in the country.

Argentine real estate creates a specific local route

An apartment, house, land or other immovable asset in Argentina is not transferred merely because heirs were recognised abroad. The local title and succession record must support registration in the heirs’ names or a later sale. This does not necessarily mean that Argentina should administer every foreign asset in the estate; the Argentine connection may be limited to the local property.

Movable and financial assets require a separate assessment

Bank accounts, vehicles, shares, receivables and company interests should not be treated automatically like real estate. Their location, registration, account terms, the deceased’s domicile, treaty rules and the relief available from the competent court can differ. An inventory should identify the legal owner and institution rather than listing only an estimated value.

Foreign probate records may be evidence but not the final step

A foreign grant, declaration of heirs or probate judgment may help prove status and history. Whether it needs recognition, can be relied on incidentally or should be accompanied by an Argentine succession proceeding depends on the document and the local asset. The foreign order should be reviewed before assuming that exequatur is either always required or always sufficient.

Documents should be checked before apostille and translation

Common records include the death certificate, birth or marriage certificates connecting each heir, the will, foreign probate decisions and proof of finality. Names and dates should be consistent. Authentication depends on the issuing country and applicable convention; an Apostille may replace diplomatic legalisation only when the Apostille Convention operates between the States. Documents used in an Argentine court generally require Spanish translation by a registered public translator under the applicable procedural rules.

Remote representation is often possible, but the power must fit the task

A properly prepared power of attorney may allow Argentine counsel to file, obtain reports and perform authorised procedural acts. Powers to accept an inheritance, settle, partition, sell property or receive funds may require more specific wording or formalities than a general litigation power. The draft should be reviewed for Argentine use before it is signed abroad.

Define the practical objective before opening proceedings

The immediate goal may be to preserve an asset, obtain recognition as an heir, administer it, partition the estate or sell. That choice affects the reports, consents, powers, tax information and registration steps required. A focused first review is more useful than collecting every historical family document without knowing which Argentine result is needed.

Did the deceased leave an asset in Argentina?

For a useful first review, identify the deceased’s last domicile, date of death, each known Argentine asset, the claimed heirs, any will and any foreign probate decision. We can then assess whether local succession, recognition or another Argentine step appears appropriate.

Legal framework considered
  • Argentine Civil and Commercial Code, arts. 2335–2338 and 2643–2645.
  • The procedural and registration rules of the competent Argentine jurisdiction.
  • 1961 Apostille Convention (approved by Argentine Law 23,458), when it is in force between the relevant States.
Official sources
  • Argentine Civil and Commercial Code
  • Argentine Code of Civil and Commercial Procedure
  • Argentine law approving the Apostille Convention

This article provides general legal information about Argentina. It does not replace advice based on the facts, dates, competent jurisdiction and treaty relationship of a specific case. Foreign-law questions must be reviewed by counsel qualified in the relevant foreign jurisdiction. Representation may also require counsel admitted in the competent Argentine jurisdiction.
